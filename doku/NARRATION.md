# Narration Script — "How Hitler Came to Power" (English)

Opening (0:00–0:33) has **no narration**: Hitler's Krupp speech (original German, English subtitles) → title card.
Narration starts at ~0:33. Target: ~1,250 words ≈ 8:45 at a calm documentary pace → total ≈ 9:20.

Lines marked *(interpretation)* are presented as historians' assessment, not as fact.

---

## 0:33 — Bridge
The man you just heard is Adolf Hitler, speaking to steelworkers in Essen. By then, Germany had no free elections, no free press and no opposition. Yet only three years earlier, Germany had been a democracy. How could that democracy be destroyed so quickly? The answer begins in November 1918.

## Chapter 1 — Defeat and Revolution (1918–1919)
November 1918. After four years of war, Germany is defeated. Sailors mutiny in Kiel, and revolution spreads across the country. On the ninth of November the Kaiser abdicates, and a republic is proclaimed from the Reichstag in Berlin. Two days later, the guns fall silent.

In January 1919, Germans elect a national assembly — and for the first time, women can vote. In Weimar, it writes one of the most democratic constitutions of its time. But it also contains Article 48: emergency powers for the president.

In June 1919 comes the Treaty of Versailles. Germany loses territory, its army is limited, and it must pay reparations. Many Germans feel humiliated. Nationalists spread a lie: that the army was never beaten on the battlefield, but "stabbed in the back" by democrats, socialists and Jews. The young republic is born carrying the blame for a defeat it did not cause.

## Chapter 2 — The Early Nazi Movement (1919–1923)
In Munich, in September 1919, a former army corporal named Adolf Hitler attends a meeting of a tiny nationalist group, the German Workers' Party. He joins — and soon discovers his talent as a speaker.

In 1920 the party is renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party: the NSDAP. From the very beginning, its programme is built on radical nationalism and hatred of Jews. In 1921 Hitler becomes its leader. Its stormtroopers, the SA, bring violence to the streets.

Then, 1923. French and Belgian troops occupy the Ruhr. The German currency collapses. By November, one US dollar costs four point two trillion marks. Savings are wiped out overnight.

Hitler believes his moment has come. On the eighth of November 1923, he storms a beer hall in Munich and declares a national revolution. The next day, his march through the city is stopped by police gunfire. Four policemen and sixteen Nazis are killed. The Beer Hall Putsch has failed.

## Chapter 3 — A New Strategy (1924–1929)
Hitler is put on trial for high treason. Sympathetic judges let him turn the courtroom into a stage. He is sentenced to five years — and released after less than one. In prison, he writes Mein Kampf, a book full of hatred, conquest and antisemitism.

His lesson: take power legally, through elections — while despising the democracy that holds them.

Meanwhile, the republic recovers: a stable currency, American loans — the "Golden Twenties." In May 1928, the Nazi Party wins just two point six percent of the vote. At this moment, Hitler's rise looks anything but inevitable.

## Chapter 4 — The Great Depression (1929–1932)
Then, in October 1929, the New York stock market crashes. American loans are called back. German factories close. By early 1932, around six million Germans are registered as unemployed.

The government collapses. Chancellor Heinrich Brüning rules by emergency decree under Article 48, pushing parliament aside.

The Nazis offer simple answers and scapegoats. Joseph Goebbels builds a modern propaganda machine: posters, films, mass rallies. Hitler flies from city to city, speaking to several crowds a day. On the streets, Nazis and Communists fight pitched battles. Hundreds are killed.

The results are dramatic. September 1930: eighteen point three percent. July 1932: thirty-seven point three percent. The Nazis are now the largest party in the Reichstag. But in November 1932, they fall back to thirty-three point one percent — and lose two million votes.

This is the crucial fact: the Nazi Party never won a majority in a free national election. Two out of three voters chose other parties.

## Chapter 5 — The Backroom Deal (January 1933)
By late 1932, the Nazi movement seems to have peaked.

But a small circle of conservative politicians decides otherwise. Former chancellor Franz von Papen wants to return to power. On the fourth of January 1933, he meets Hitler secretly in Cologne. Together with the nationalist leader Alfred Hugenberg, they persuade the eighty-five-year-old President, Paul von Hindenburg, to drop Chancellor Kurt von Schleicher.

Their plan is to use Hitler's mass movement — and keep him under control. Only three Nazis will sit in the new cabinet. Papen boasts that within two months, they will have pushed Hitler into a corner.

On the thirtieth of January 1933, Hindenburg appoints Adolf Hitler Reich Chancellor. He has not won an election. He has been handed power. That night, the SA marches with torches through the Brandenburg Gate.

## Chapter 6 — Democracy Destroyed (February–March 1933)
Hitler calls new elections at once. In Prussia, Hermann Göring makes the SA and SS auxiliary police — the thugs now wear the badge of the state.

On the night of the twenty-seventh of February, the Reichstag is on fire. A young Dutch communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, is arrested inside. Who was behind the fire is still debated *(interpretation)* — but the Nazis use it at once.

The very next day, Hindenburg signs the Reichstag Fire Decree. Freedom of speech, of the press and of assembly are suspended "until further notice." Thousands of Communists and Social Democrats are arrested.

On the fifth of March, Germans vote under terror. Even now, the Nazis win only forty-three point nine percent. Near Munich, one of the first concentration camps opens: Dachau.

On the twenty-third of March, the Reichstag meets in the Kroll Opera House. SA and SS men line the hall. The Communist deputies are already in prison or in hiding. Hitler demands an Enabling Act: the right to pass laws without parliament. Only one party votes no — the Social Democrats. Their leader, Otto Wels, declares: "You can take our freedom and our lives, but not our honour." The vote: four hundred forty-four to ninety-four. Parliament has voted itself out of existence.

## Chapter 7 — The Dictatorship (1933–1934)
Now everything happens fast. In April, Jewish shops are boycotted and Jews are driven out of public service. In May, trade unions are smashed, and books are burned in public squares. By July, every party except the NSDAP has been banned or has dissolved itself. Germany is a one-party state.

Opponents disappear into camps, watched over by a new secret police: the Gestapo.

In the summer of 1934, Hitler turns even on his own followers. In the "Night of the Long Knives," SA leader Ernst Röhm and at least eighty-five people in all — among them former chancellor Schleicher — are murdered without trial.

On the second of August 1934, President Hindenburg dies. Hitler merges the offices of president and chancellor. Soldiers now swear an oath of loyalty — not to the constitution, but to Hitler personally. Five hundred and forty-nine days after his appointment, his dictatorship is complete.

## Epilogue
Hitler's rise was not inevitable. He never won a majority. He was given power by men who thought they could control him — and then used the law, propaganda and violence to destroy democracy from within.

What followed was a war that cost tens of millions of lives, and the Holocaust: the murder of six million Jews.

Democracy did not die in a single day. It was dismantled — step by step.
